09 — Model selection

Which model is greenest for this workflow?

The greenest model is not the smallest one — it is the smallest one that clears the quality bar, because a model that fails the task pays full price twice. Describe the workflow, and every candidate is costed on money, carbon and capability together.

Why the abatement figures are so large. At realistic volumes the money saved by switching models outweighs the carbon saved by three to five orders of magnitude in dollar terms. That is not a rounding error — it is the central strategic fact on this page. No plausible carbon price will ever drive model selection, so do not build the business case on carbon. Build it on cost, and bank the emissions reduction as a consequence. Sustainability programmes that depend on a carbon price to justify themselves lose the budget argument; cost-reduction programmes that happen to cut emissions survive it.

Calculation logic — every term, in order
  1. Effective attempts. A model one capability tier below the workflow requirement is assumed to need one extra attempt; two tiers below, two extra. A = 1 + max(0, tier_required − tier_model). This is what stops the cheapest model winning by default.
  2. Cost per task. $ = (T_in · P_in + T_out · P_out) ÷ 1,000,000 × calls × A, where calls is 1 for chat-style work and higher for agentic archetypes.
  3. Energy per task. Wh = (T_out · d + T_in · p) × calls × A, using the decode and prefill coefficients for the model's size class from section 03.
  4. Carbon per task. gCO₂e = Wh ÷ 1000 × PUE × I_grid, taking PUE and grid intensity from your settings in section 01.
  5. Annualised. Multiply both by tasks per month, then by twelve.
  6. Marginal abatement cost. MAC = (Cost_model − Cost_baseline) ÷ (CO₂_baseline − CO₂_model), in dollars per kilogram of CO₂e avoided. A negative MAC means the switch saves money and carbon at once — a no-regret move that needs no sustainability budget to justify. A positive MAC is the real price of that abatement, and should be compared against your internal carbon price before you claim it as a green initiative.
  7. Green score. The five dimensions from section 05, recomputed per model with the workflow's token profile and the attempt penalty applied.

Two warnings before this drives a procurement decision. First, list prices move constantly and the defaults here reflect published rates around August 2026 — verify every row against the provider's current rate card, and note that batch processing and prompt caching can each cut the cost column substantially without changing the carbon column much. Second, the capability tiers are a coarse judgement, not a benchmark result: the honest version of this table is one where you have run your own evaluation set and replaced the tier column with your own pass rates.

This agent is deterministic, not generative. 12 — Equilibrium

In the race to adopt AI, are we missing the environment?

Partly, and in a specific way. The environment is not being ignored — it is being measured on the wrong axis. Efficiency per task is celebrated because it is improving spectacularly. Total load is downplayed because efficiency is improving spectacularly. Both are reported honestly and the combination is misleading, because the quantity that decides the outcome is neither one alone: it is whether workload growth, net of efficiency, outruns the growth of clean supply.

12.1

How far can the environment sustain this?

A carrying-capacity model with three variables. Workload volume grows. Efficiency cuts the energy each unit of work needs. Clean supply grows to meet what is left. Equilibrium holds only while the first two, combined, stay below the third.

Net demand growth  =  (1 + g) · (1 − e) − 1
Equilibrium holds while   (1 + g) · (1 − e)  ≤  (1 + c)
g = workload growth  ·  e = efficiency gain  ·  c = clean supply growth

12.2

What holds the equilibrium

Each condition below moves one variable in the equation above. The order is deliberate — it runs from what you control this week to what takes a decade and a parliament.

Bend g before you polish eWorkload growth · immediate · yours

Efficiency is losing a race it did not choose. At the default settings the industry needs a far larger annual efficiency gain than it is achieving, and the gap is made of workload volume, not model design. Cap agent step counts, route to small models by default, kill the retry tax. This is the only variable that responds in days.

Keep e compounding, and stop claiming it as victoryEfficiency · continuous · engineering

Quantisation, distillation, sparsity, better serving stacks and speculative decoding have delivered genuine order-of-magnitude gains and must continue. But an efficiency gain announced without the corresponding volume figure is a half-truth, and the half that is missing is the one that decides the answer.

Raise c with additional generation, not certificatesClean supply · years · procurement and policy

Buying existing clean electricity displaces another consumer onto fossil generation and changes nothing at system level. Only additional capacity, contracted ahead of the load it serves, moves c. Hourly matching makes the claim auditable; annual certificates do not.

Decouple the timing, not just the totalDemand flexibility · months · operations

A crossover is a peak problem before it is an annual problem. Grid-interactive data centres that curtail or shift training and batch inference into clean hours buy real years of runway without reducing a single unit of work done.

Measure, or forfeit the argumentDisclosure · precondition for everything above

None of these variables can be managed at the values they actually hold, because nobody publishes them. Independent estimates for the same model differ by an order of magnitude. Until per-model energy telemetry is standard disclosure, equilibrium is a claim, not a measurement — and this framework is a well-reasoned guess.

The honest summary. The environment can sustain AI adoption indefinitely at some rate. The question was never whether, but at what rate — and the current rate is set by capital allocation and product ambition, with the environmental constraint discovered afterwards through interconnection queues, water permits and local opposition rather than chosen deliberately in advance. Equilibrium is available. It is simply not what anyone is currently optimising for.

14 — Outlook

Predicted effects on the world environment

Electricity — the load is real, the share is smaller than the discourse suggests
  • Global data centre demand sits near 485 TWh in 2025 and is projected to roughly double to 945–950 TWh by 2030 — about 3% of world electricity. AI-focused facilities are the fastest-growing slice, projected to triple over the same period.
  • Data centre demand grew 17% in 2025 against 3% growth in overall electricity demand. The pressure is a rate-of-change problem, not yet a share-of-total problem.
  • Efficiency is improving unusually fast — power per AI task has been falling by roughly an order of magnitude a year. This is the strongest argument against straight-line extrapolation, and the strongest argument for Jevons-effect caution.
  • Net expectation to 2030: a genuine new load comparable to a mid-sized industrial economy, arriving faster than grids can decarbonise, but not a dominant driver of global emissions on its own.
Carbon — siting decides the outcome more than usage does
  • The same query emits roughly eighteen times more CO₂e on a coal-heavy grid than a hydro-dominant one. Where inference runs matters more than how often it runs.
  • Natural gas and coal are expected to meet a large share of the additional data centre demand to 2030, because new capacity is being built faster than clean generation can be connected.
  • Renewables still supply the largest slice of the growth, and small modular reactors enter the mix after 2030 — but the near-term gap is filled by whatever the local grid has spare.
  • Net expectation: AI's own emissions are modest at global scale; its indirect effect on the pace of grid decarbonisation, through interconnection queues and fossil plant retirement deferrals, is the larger risk.
Water and land — a local problem, not a global one
  • Water impact is highly concentrated. A single large facility can draw hundreds of thousands of gallons a day, which is trivial nationally and severe in a stressed basin.
  • Concentration is the mechanism: nearly half of global data centre electricity is consumed in the United States, with heavy clustering in a handful of regions. Ireland already dedicates around a fifth of national electricity to data centres.
  • Net expectation: expect local grid stress, water permitting fights and land-use disputes to become the binding constraint on AI expansion well before global carbon accounting does.
The rebound risk — efficiency is not the same as reduction
  • Per-task energy is falling sharply while total consumption rises sharply. Both statements are true simultaneously, and each is used selectively by opposing camps.
  • Agentic workloads are the mechanism. A single agent run can consume tens to hundreds of times a chat query, because it issues many model calls and carries long context through each one.
  • Net expectation: the dominant environmental variable for the rest of this decade is not model efficiency but workload design — how many calls a task makes, and whether anyone is counting.

Where this framework is weak, stated plainly. Per-query energy figures are estimates, not measurements — no major lab publishes full telemetry, and independent estimates for the same model vary by an order of magnitude. Embodied carbon in chips, buildings and networks is excluded from the per-query figures and folded into a single crude amplifier. Water intensity varies by site and season far more than any single coefficient can capture. Treat every number here as a decision aid with wide error bars, not an audit result.

15 — Method

How the numbers are derived

Calculation chain
  • Energy is built from tokens, not from a flat per-query constant. Each model class carries a decode coefficient in watt-hours per output token and a prefill coefficient per input token, set roughly thirty times lower because prefill batches efficiently while decode runs the whole model once per token.
  • Calibration. The mid-tier coefficient reproduces a median near 0.31 Wh for a typical query, matching peer-reviewed estimates for optimised large-scale inference with an interquartile range of about 0.16–0.60 Wh. Published per-token measurements cluster at 0.0001–0.002 Wh, which brackets every coefficient used here.
  • Reasoning models add hidden thinking tokens to the decode count. Agentic runs multiply by sixteen internal model invocations, each re-reading the carried context — which is why context length hurts agents far more than it hurts chat.
  • Retries multiply the whole chain linearly — a failed answer costs full price.
  • Facility energy = IT energy × PUE. Carbon = facility kWh × grid intensity. Water = facility kWh × (on-site cooling coefficient + off-site generation coefficient scaled to grid carbon intensity).
  • Score bands each dimension against the rubric in section 05 and sums to 100.
